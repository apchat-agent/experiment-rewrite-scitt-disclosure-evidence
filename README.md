# experiment-rewrite-scitt-disclosure-evidence

An experiment in rewriting an IETF Internet-Draft into more accessible prose.

## How this was made

This is an experiment in using **Claude (an LLM) as a tool to simplify the
language of specifications for human reading**, taking industry standards for
controlled technical English into account.

To be explicit, because the point of the experiment is undermined otherwise:

- `draft-rewritten.md` was **generated by Claude**, under human direction,
  against the rules in `rewrite-rules-wip.md`.
- `rewrite-rules-wip.md` was derived by a human and Claude together, over one
  afternoon, and tested on real text as it went.
- `rewrite-report.md` is **Claude's own account** of what it changed, what it
  refused to change, and the 15 places it believes it may have altered meaning.

## What this is

On 2026-08-22 we took [`draft-dogru-scitt-disclosure-evidence-04`][draft] and
rewrote its Abstract and Sections 1–9 under a set of readability rules, to see
what a specification looks like when the register is changed and nothing else is.

It is an experiment about **prose**, not about the specification's content. We
picked this draft because it is *well made* —
its Implementation Status section lists in detail what its implementation does
*not* do, and it explicitly refuses to report a bare pass. Nothing here is a criticism of the
work.

[draft]: https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/draft-dogru-scitt-disclosure-evidence/

## Why

Technical prose can be fluent, grammatical, confident — and still hard to read.
That excludes people: readers in a second language, readers short of time,
readers outside the working group. It is a real cost to a consensus process, and
it is not the same problem as bad writing.

## Result

| | median sentence | p90 | over 25 words | words |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| original (Abstract + §1–9) | 22 | 39 | 42 sentences | 5,765 |
| rewritten | 12 | 20 | 1 sentence* | 6,465 |

Sentence statistics are our own, computed with a simple regex splitter; no
tool ships with this repo, so treat them as indicative.

\* The one remaining long sentence is the BCP 14 keyword boilerplate, which
cannot be edited without changing the normative binding of every keyword in the
document. Our own splitter reports a second hit, which is an artefact of it
joining an italic note to the following sentence; `rewrite-report.md`, using a
different splitter, reports one. Neither is wrong. Sentence counts are method-dependent —
hard-wrapped definition lists split differently depending on the tool — so treat
these as our numbers, not as the numbers.

The rewrite is **12% longer**. That is the expected direction: plain words need
more of them, and "shorter" was never the goal.

Normative content was checked mechanically for parity. Counting bare keywords
(so MUST excludes MUST NOT): MUST 16, MUST NOT 23, SHOULD 2, SHOULD NOT 1, MAY 4,
SHALL 1, SHALL NOT 1, REQUIRED 1, RECOMMENDED 3, OPTIONAL 1 — identical in both
texts, all ten classes. Defined terms and outcome names are
unchanged.

## Read the report before trusting the rewrite

`rewrite-report.md` lists 15 places where meaning may have shifted, worst first.
The top item is genuine: the source phrase *"a bare pass"* has two defensible
readings, and the rewrite commits to one of them in three places. Those items
need a human reading against the source. That part cannot be automated, and we
have not done it.

## What we learned, briefly

The rules that survived testing came from [ASD-STE100][ste] (Simplified Technical
English), the aerospace maintenance standard — built because fluent-but-dense
English excluded competent technicians reading a second language from
safety-critical work.

Things that did **not** survive testing, each recorded in `rewrite-rules-wip.md`:

- An absolute information-density threshold **inverted**: a padded, unimproved
  version passed it while a genuine rewrite failed.
- Nominalisation counting scored a good rewrite **worse** than the original.
- A general controlled vocabulary **collides** with a specification's own defined
  terms.
- Short sentences built from common words used in specialist senses pass every
  mechanical check and are still unreadable.

For calibration: RFC 2131 (DHCP) breaks the 25-word sentence rule in 30% of its
sentences. An absolute rule would condemn the entire RFC series, so we do not
propose one.

[ste]: https://www.asd-ste100.org/
